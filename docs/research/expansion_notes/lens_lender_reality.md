# Lens: does the lender actually want a drought hedge?

Research track, 2026-11-20. Stress-tests shortlist #1 in [doc 25](../../analysis/25_weather_hedging_expansion.md), the "wholesale Companion": Texas, Oklahoma and Missouri ag lenders, grazing co-ops and hay buyers buy the Companion's Nov-Feb Kalshi rain hedges, hold them against their book, pool farm-level basis risk, and recover the premium at sale. Every number has a URL in the table at the end. "Inference" marks the team's reasoning, not a source.

## Verdict on shortlist #1

**Reframe it. As written ("the lender holds the contracts against its loan book") it fails the lender's own test.**

1. **There is almost no drought loss on the lender's book to hedge.** In the three test droughts, charge-offs fell or stayed near zero, and the largest Texas Farm Credit association posted strong or record earnings. Commercial banks' ag-production charge-off rate was 0.24% in 2011 and 0.25% in 2012, against 0.77% in 2010 (a recession year). Capital Farm Credit's net charge-offs were 0.2% in 2011 (down from 0.6%) and 0.0% in 2022 and 2023, with record net income of $286M in 2022. Loans are mostly land-secured (80.7% real estate mortgage at Capital Farm Credit in 2011). Borrowers carry MPCI, PRF and free federal disaster programs, and bankers in 2011 and 2022 said insurance was paying down lines.
2. **A hedge against a near-zero exposure is a position, not a hedge.** Pooling basis risk across borrowers only helps when the lender has a loss to offset. FCA calls speculative derivative use "unsafe and unsound" (BL-023, via exam manual EM-61.3). Associations may hold only US-government securities as risk-management investments without case-by-case FCA approval (12 CFR 615.5140). Exchange-traded weather contracts also get mark-to-market derivative accounting.
3. **What the lender actually loses in a drought is volume, not principal.** Operating lines don't get drawn, cattle loans are prepaid as herds are sold, and production books shrink (Capital Farm Credit's production portfolio fell 13.5% in 2011). Two years later come repayment slippage and carryover debt (Dallas Fed, 2023 onward). That motive is real but small: about 2% of 2011 net income by the team's estimate (inference). A rain contract that pays the lender does not restore loan demand.
4. **What survives is the lender as a distributor and premium financier, not as the hedger.** Farm Credit associations already sell PRF, earn income from it, and "ramped up" insurance sales in the 2023 drought. The Casaburi & Willis pay-at-harvest lever works when the financier controls sale proceeds. A US lender's operating line and lien on cattle sale proceeds can do that (inference). The borrower holds the contract, and the lender finances the premium as ordinary credit. That keeps the loan-book story out of it, but it brings back retail onboarding.
5. **The strongest wholesale *principal* buyer of dry protection is the PRF insurer (AIP), not the lender.** AIPs really do lose money in droughts: Texas PRF paid $247M on $181M of premium in 2022, a loss ratio of 1.37. Under the SRA, insurers share losses on the business they retain. Their exposure sits in the intervals farmers chose, though, so the product would be a book-level reinsurance-style hedge, not the Companion's gap months (inference).

Recommended change to doc 25: replace "lender holds contracts against its book" with two separate pitches. (a) **Lender/co-op as distribution and premium financing for the borrower-level Companion.** The motives are retention, operating-line volume and insurance income. (b) **AIP book hedge as the wholesale principal buyer.** Drop "grazing co-ops", which barely exist in TX/OK/MO. Recast "hay buyers" as dairies and feedlots, whose drought exposure is to feed cost, not rain at an airport.

## Findings

### 1. Do US ag lenders lose money in droughts? Mostly no, and not when the drought happens

**National, commercial banks (FRED, quarterly averages by year, team computation from the FRED CSVs).**

| Year | Charge-off rate, ag production loans, all banks | Same, banks outside top 100 | Delinquency rate, ag production loans |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2009 | 0.49% | 0.41% | 2.40% |
| 2010 | 0.77% | 0.30% | 3.03% |
| **2011 (TX/OK drought)** | 0.24% | 0.12% | 2.10% |
| **2012 (national drought)** | 0.25% | 0.07% | 1.57% |
| 2013 | 0.04% | 0.04% | 1.15% |
| **2022 (TX/OK drought)** | 0.03% | 0.03% | 0.98% |
| **2023** | 0.08% | 0.05% | 0.80% |
| 2024 | 0.20% | 0.07% | 1.10% |

Drought years are among the *lowest*-loss years in the series. Losses track the business cycle (2009-10) and the 2016-2020 low-price farm recession far more than rain.

**Farm Credit System, national.** Net charge-offs fell from $596M in 2010 to $500M in 2011 and $236M in 2012, "the most widespread drought in 50 years". Nonperforming loans fell from 1.72% to 1.36% of gross loans, and delinquencies fell from 0.38% to 0.28% ([FCA 2011](https://www.fca.gov/template-fca/download/AnnualReports/2011AnnualReport.pdf), [FCA 2012](https://www.fca.gov/template-fca/download/AnnualReports/2012AnnualReport.pdf)). FCA's explanation is that drought-region producers with crop insurance "fared well" and that 2012 crop indemnities were a record $16.2B. The stressed segment was livestock, which had "limited insurance". Even so, FCS cattle charge-offs were only $43M on $16.5B in 2011 (about 0.26%, team computation) and $16M in 2012.

**Texas Farm Credit, 2011.** The Texas Farm Credit District's net charge-offs were 0.60% of average loans in 2011, down from 0.75% in 2010. Net income was $368.7M, up from $275.3M. The district's provision fell 68.2%. It says drought losses were "mitigated by crop insurance" and that ranchers sold herds into strong cattle prices "and capture profit" ([FCBT 2011 district report](https://www.farmcreditbank.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/2011-district-annual-report.pdf)). Capital Farm Credit, the largest Texas association, had livestock as 58.6% of its portfolio and Texas herds down 38%. Its net charge-offs were 0.2% (2010: 0.6%, 2009: 0.7%), its provision was $2.2M versus $29.2M, and its net income was $126.8M ([CFC 2011](https://www.capitalfarmcredit.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/cfc-2011-annual-report.pdf)). Its 2011 charge-offs came from transitional real estate, timber, dairy and feedlots.

**Texas Farm Credit, 2022-2023.** Capital Farm Credit had net charge-offs of 0.0% in both years and net income of $286.3M (2022, a record) and $270.3M (2023). Loans classified acceptable were 98.4% and 97.3%. It also reported "the largest cow herd liquidation in Texas since 2011" ([CFC 2022](https://www.capitalfarmcredit.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/cfc-2022-annual-report.pdf), [CFC 2023](https://www.capitalfarmcredit.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/cfc-2023-annual-report.pdf)).

**What the bankers said (Dallas Fed, 11th District, Q3 2011).** The rate-of-repayment index was *positive* (+9.5) and renewals were flat (-3.4), while loan demand hit -36.2, "its lowest level in the recent past". Bankers' comments included "farmers were using crop insurance payments to pay down loans", "Credit lines have not funded, and interest revenues are down", "term loans to purchase the breeding herd are prepaid" and "from a credit standpoint, we are in good shape due to insurance". One coastal banker noted that "there is no substantial insurance for the ranchers". Dry cropland and ranchland values "held fairly steady" ([Dallas Fed 2011 Q3](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/frbdal_ags/frbdal_agSurvey_2011_q3.pdf)). In Q3 2022, repayment was +11.6, renewals -5.9 and demand -27.1, and one banker said crop insurance was "a very, very good secondary source of repayment" ([Dallas Fed 2022 Q3](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/surveys/agsurvey/2022/ag2203)). Stress arrived later. Renewals turned positive in Q2 2023 (+3.9) after nine falling quarters, and repayment slipped (-1.3) ([Dallas Fed 2023 Q2](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/surveys/agsurvey/2023/ag2302)).

**Oklahoma.** In KC Fed's Q3 2022 survey, about 60% of Oklahoma bankers reported a significant drought impact, the most of any state. Oklahoma was the only state where the share of loans with repayment problems rose, and then only "slightly". District-wide, about 6% of loan volume was on watch lists and about 3% was classified ([KC Fed Q3 2022](https://www.kansascityfed.org/agriculture/ag-credit-survey/strong-farm-economy-but-interest-expenses-and-drought-intensify-pressures)).

**Research.** FDIC staff find that a one-standard-deviation rise in county crop indemnities lowers local banks' ag past-dues by 6 bp, about a fifth of the series' time variation. They also show that production shortfalls show up as *carryover debt* restructured into farmland-secured loans, not as production-loan losses. Charge-offs come after several bad years ([Heil, Pogach & Polson, FDIC CFR WP 2024-05](https://www.fdic.gov/center-financial-research/agricultural-lending-insurance-and-implications-climate-change)). NY Fed staff find that banks lent more in better-insured counties and that insurance compensated them "such that their overall risk did not increase meaningfully" ([Kahn, Panjwani & Santos, SR 1107](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1107.pdf)).

**The lender's real motive, ranked (inference from the above).**
1. *Loan volume and interest income.* Operating lines go unused and cattle loans are prepaid. Capital Farm Credit's production and intermediate-term loans fell from $523.4M to $452.9M in 2011, which is -$70.5M or -13.5%. At its 3.4% net interest margin that is about $2.4M of foregone net interest income, roughly 1.9% of 2011 net income (team computation).
2. *Borrower retention, competition and fee income.* Associations sell PRF and crop insurance. Capital Farm Credit "ramped up" insurance in the 2023 drought, "generating additional income".
3. *Carryover debt and repayment slippage one to three years later* (Dallas Fed 2023-25; FDIC WP).
4. *Regulatory capital* is not binding: FCBT district equity was 15.8% of assets in 2011, and Capital Farm Credit's ROA was 2.5%.

A Nov-Feb rain contract paying the lender does not fix 1 or 3. It could fund 2 as a marketing cost.

### 2. Are lenders already "hedged" through the borrower? Largely yes

- **FSA (USDA direct loans)** requires at least CAT crop insurance on each basic crop and an assignment of indemnity to the agency, and requires insurance on growing crops used as security ([7 CFR 764.108](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/7/764.108)).
- **Commercial banks.** In the latest found survey (ABA, 2000), 49% of banks required crop insurance (44% in 1999). The figures were 94% for banks serving cotton farmers, 35% for dairy, 57% for banks under $50M in assets and 35% for those over $300M ([Murray, J. Agricultural Lending 2001](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/336591/files/ers2022-23-0549.pdf)). No current figure was found, and no source says lenders *require* PRF.
- **Farm Credit associations sell the insurance themselves.** 12 CFR 618.8040 permits them to sell hail and multi-peril crop insurance. Staff may not take commissions personally, but the institution earns the income ([12 CFR 618.8040](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/618.8040)). Capital Farm Credit sells PRF and carried a $1.4M crop-insurance commission receivable at year-end 2023.
- **Implication (inference).** The lender's drought exposure is already transferred to federally subsidized insurance its borrowers buy, often from the lender. What is left is the uninsured tail: ranchers without PRF, uninsured months, and feed-cost spikes ("no substantial insurance for the ranchers"). That tail is the Companion's actual target. It sits with the *borrower*, which argues for the lender as distribution channel, not as policyholder.
- **A warning from RMA (2026).** Third-party arrangements that pay a PRF premium in exchange for a share of indemnities violate the anti-rebating rule (FCIA 508(a)(9)). AIPs must report them, and several cases have gone to USDA OIG ([RMA COM-26-003](https://www.rma.usda.gov/policy-procedure/bulletins-memos/informational-memorandum/com-26-003-rainfall-index-ri-pasture)). The Companion is not federal insurance. Still, a lender that sells PRF and also gives borrowers a free or deferred-cost Kalshi hedge would need counsel on inducement and rebating optics (inference).

### 3. Precedents: lender-held weather index covers exist only abroad, and only with donor support

- **United States.** No US lender, co-op or hay buyer was found buying weather-index protection on its own book. A search for US "weather-index-linked loans" found none. The nearest are Cornell's risk-contingent-credit concept project and sustainability-linked loans ([USDA NIFA project](https://portal.nifa.usda.gov/web/crisprojectpages/0208542-financially-engineered-credit-instruments-for-agriculture-and-agribusiness.html)). The OCC did approve a bank acting as a *matched intermediary* in natural-catastrophe index swaps in 2008. That covered fees only, with the bank "not obligated on any of the underlying risks", and required an examiner no-objection ([OCC IL 1101](https://www.occ.gov/topics/charters-and-licensing/interpretations-and-actions/2008/int1101.pdf)).
- **Peru, El Niño insurance (GlobalAgRisk, Gates and UNDP funded).** This was an index on Nov-Dec Niño 1.2 sea-surface temperature that paid in January, before the floods ([GlobalAgRisk briefing 2011](https://www.eird.org/plataforma-2011/docs/16demarzo/RRD-sector-privado-16-de-marzo/GlobalAgRisk_UNDP_ProjectBriefing.pdf)). It was the closest analogue to a lender book cover. Collier & Skees report that "a large MFI in Peru… is now managing its disaster risk using El Niño insurance" ([Collier & Skees 2012](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/125535/files/Portfolio%20Analysis%20FI%20Final%20IAAE.pdf)). The lender profile is the *opposite* of a Texas Farm Credit association: uncollateralized microloans, 30-50% of the portfolio in the exposed north, 1998 floods at about 40 times normal rainfall, and capital ratios converging to the 14% regulatory floor. A reported purchase by Caja Nuestra Gente (585 loans, $4.6M, 2012) came only from a search summary and could not be confirmed (unverified). Peru later moved to government-bought catastrophe cover ([Artemis](https://www.artemis.bm/news/peru-buys-catastrophe-insurance-to-protect-farmers-from-el-nino-losses/)). No evidence was found of whether the lender policy ever paid. The 2017 coastal El Niño developed abruptly in Jan-Mar, after a Nov-Dec trigger window ([Springer, 2017 event](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13753-017-0151-8)). Whether it triggered is unverified, and it is a possible basis-timing case.
- **Haiti, MiCRO and Fonkoze.** A parametric cover reinsured by Swiss Re and funded by Mercy Corps paid about $1.64M to about 6,800 Fonkoze borrowers in its first year. After Hurricane Sandy it paid $125 each plus loan cancellation ([CS Monitor](https://m.csmonitor.com/World/Making-a-difference/Change-Agent/2012/0907/Tropical-Storm-Isaac-triggers-microinsurance-payment-to-Haiti-s-budding-entrepreneurs); [Artemis](https://www.artemis.bm/news/hurricane-sandy-triggers-fonkoze-micro-weather-index-microinsurance-in-haiti)). It worked, but as a donor-built facility for borrowers with no collateral.
- **India, BASIX/ICICI Lombard 2003.** BASIX sold the first rainfall-index policies, about 230 of them, as distributor, not as the insured party ([Giné, Townsend & Vickery, NY Fed SR 302](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr302.pdf); count from search summary). A MicroSave case is titled "Good Beginning, Sad End" ([MicroSave](https://www.microsave.net/wp-content/uploads/2013/12/Microinsurance_Towards_De_Risking_Disasters_MicroSave.pdf)); not read in full.
- **Mexico, CADENA.** State governments, not lenders, bought index cover for smallholders, with federal and state governments sharing the premium ([World Bank brief](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/124521468287160777/pdf/881000BRI0P1300urance04Pager0Cadena.pdf)). It was later discontinued ([Springer 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11027-025-10265-2)).
- **Rabobank.** Only a development-arm partnership with Achmea for African smallholders was found ([Rabobank](https://www.rabobank.com/about-us/rabo-partnerships/news/011384253/achmea-rp-build-solution-to-protect-smallholders-harvest-in-africa)). No book-level cover was found for its developed-market farm loans.
- **Pattern (inference).** Lender-held index cover appears where loans are unsecured, the lender is undercapitalized and geographically concentrated, the peril is catastrophic and rare, and a donor pays for development. Texas Farm Credit fails every one of those conditions.

### 4. Regulatory angle

- **Farm Credit associations.** EM-61.3 defines derivatives to include contracts on "the occurrence or nonoccurrence of a specified event", which fits a Kalshi contract. Derivatives must serve board-approved purposes. Speculative use, and use "in a manner that increases risks", is "an unsafe and unsound practice". Associations need policies under 12 CFR 615.5182, and customer-facing derivative services must be hedged and "substantially offset" ([FCA EM-61.3](https://ww3.fca.gov/readingrm/exammanual/New%20Exam%20Manual/61.3.pdf)). Risk-management *investments* by associations are limited to US-government securities and USDA-guaranteed loan portions. They require the funding bank's prior approval and are capped at 10% of loans. Anything else needs FCA approval under 615.5140(e) ([12 CFR 615.5140, eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-VI/subchapter-B/part-615/subpart-E/section-615.5140)).
  - *Inference.* A Kalshi position is cash-collateralized at the exchange. It is closer to a derivative than an eligible investment, so it would need board policy, funding-bank sign-off and likely an FCA conversation. Hedging an exposure the association cannot show on its loss history invites the "increases risk" reading.
- **National banks.** The OCC's precedent is the matched-intermediary model (IL 1101), not principal hedging. Principal hedging of bank-permissible risk is the general OCC posture, but no letter on event contracts was found (inference). The Volcker Rule exempts banks with under $10B in assets and trading assets of 5% or less of total assets ([FDIC EGRRCPA page](https://www.fdic.gov/about/transparency-accountability-egrrcpa-s-2155-rulemakings)), which covers almost all rural ag banks.
- **Accounting.** Exchange-traded weather contracts fall *inside* ASC 815. The weather scope exception is for non-exchange-traded contracts ([PwC Viewpoint](https://viewpoint.pwc.com/dt/us/en/pwc/accounting_guides/derivatives_and_hedg/derivatives_and_hedg_US/chapter_3_scope_exce_US/32_contracts_exclude_US.html)). Positions are therefore marked through earnings, and the hedged "item" (future credit losses or lost interest) is unlikely to qualify for hedge accounting (inference).
- **ECP** matters only for OTC swaps and Kalshi block trades, not for ordinary trading on Kalshi's exchange (doc 25; [7 U.S.C. 1a(18)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/7/1a)). Institutional access is now routine: Cantor is opening Kalshi to about 3,000 institutional clients ([Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/cantor-fitzgerald-opens-kalshis-event-contracts-to-roughly-3000-institutional-clients/)).

### 5. Co-ops and hay buyers

- **Grazing co-ops** are a Northern Plains national-grasslands institution. Examples are Sugarloaf (32 members, Nebraska) and Grand River (90 members, South Dakota) ([Ag Proud](https://agproud.com/articles/57823-cooperative-grazing-associations-preserve-their-role-in-the-beef-industry)). None was found in TX, OK or MO. The closest was an informal North Texas–Southern Oklahoma grazing club ([AgriLife Vernon](https://agrilife.org/vernon/center-programs/sustainable-rangeland-management/grazing-club/)). This buyer class is close to empty in the target region.
- **Hay buyers.** No ranking of Texas hay buyers exists. USDA AMS Texas hay reports quote dairies and feedyards as the commercial buyers, and the cattle producers themselves buy in droughts ([USDA AMS Texas hay](https://www.ams.usda.gov/mnreports/ams_2707.pdf)). Panhandle hay reached $410/ton in January 2023 after the 2022 drought ([AgriLife Today](https://agrilifetoday.tamu.edu/2023/02/01/hay-supplies)). A dairy or feedlot loses through *feed prices* over a wide region, not through Nov-Feb rain at one airport. The Companion's fit for them is untested, and the commodity is not hedgeable on any exchange (inference).
- **ECP plausibility.** There are 1,647 US ag co-ops with $124.7B in total assets, of which the top 100 hold 58.6% ([USDA RD SR 87](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/366885/files/co-opSTATS-sr87.pdf)). That implies the other roughly 1,547 average about $33M (team computation). Texas hosts 134 co-op headquarters ([USDA RD bulletin](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDARD/bulletins/3bb2bc1)). A typical gin, elevator or supply co-op plausibly clears the >$10M-asset ECP test (inference). Those co-ops are cotton and grain, though, not grazing.

### 6. Who is the strongest wholesale buyer for dry protection?

| Candidate | Loses money in drought? | Sophistication / access | Fit with Companion's Nov-Feb station contracts | Verdict (inference) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ag lenders (FCS, banks) | Barely (see 1). Volume, not principal | High; FCA/OCC friction | Weak: no loss to offset | Distributor and premium financier, not principal |
| **PRF AIPs (retained SRA share)** | **Yes**: TX PRF loss ratio 1.37 in 2022 ($247M paid on $181M premium); 1.05-1.09 in 2018, 2019 and 2021 | High; already buy reinsurance; ECP | Medium: book spread over many grids averages station basis, but exposure is in *selected* intervals, not the gap months | **Strongest principal buyer**, for a book hedge of insured intervals |
| State drought funds | No US state found buying parametric ag drought cover; Mexico's CADENA was discontinued | Political | Weak | Not a near-term buyer |
| Livestock auctions | Short-run *gain*: San Angelo pens "more than twice as full" in July 2011 | Low | Opposite sign: a natural *wet-side* counterparty in the drought year | Possible counterparty, not buyer |
| Meatpackers | Multi-year national herd effect (Tyson beef losses; recovery "2028") | High | Wrong horizon and geography | No |

AIP mechanics: the SRA splits retained gains and losses with FCIC by state loss ratio and by fund, and the Assigned Risk Fund cedes most risk ([CRS R40532](https://sgp.fas.org/crs/misc/R40532.pdf)). GAO puts AIP underwriting gains at 16.8% of retained premium on average ([GAO](https://www.gao.gov/assets/870/863573.pdf); from search summary). So AIPs carry real but partly ceded PRF drought exposure, and a Kalshi book hedge competes with private reinsurance, not with subsidized PRF (inference).

### 7. Counter-evidence: interlinked credit and insurance often fails

- **Bundling lowered loan take-up in Malawi.** Take-up was 33% for an uninsured loan and 17.6% for a rainfall-insured one ([Giné & Yang 2009, J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/media/file-research-paper/insurance-credit-and-technology-adoption-field-experimental-evidence)). A World Bank summary reports that low rain led many borrowers to default *even when the index did not trigger*, which weakens repayment norms ([World Bank 2015](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/feature/2015/04/15/making-index-insurance-work-for-the-poor); search summary).
- **Mandatory bundling drove clients away in India.** When SKS required health insurance at loan renewal, renewals fell 16 percentage points, to 55%, and stayed lower after the requirement was dropped ([Banerjee, Duflo & Hornbeck 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4159356)).
- **Pay-at-harvest needs a buyer who controls the crop.** Casaburi & Willis's 72% versus 5% worked because farmers were "contractually obligated to sell their harvest to the lender". In Ethiopia, deferred payment raised take-up only from 8% to 24%, with "high default rates among some farmer groups", and co-ops "were hesitant to use their own assets to back the loan" ([J-PAL policy insight, Oct 2024](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/4519?lang=en)). For a Texas rancher selling at auction, the comparable control is the lender's lien on cattle proceeds. Hay buyers and co-ops have no such control (inference).
- **Kenyan risk-contingent credit (the conflict flagged in doc 25).** The IFPRI 2017 flyer reports 40% versus 30% loan uptake from the first, September 2017 marketing round with Equity Bank, APA and Swiss Re ([IFPRI](https://ifpri.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/fly_2017_rcc_v3_web.pdf)). The 3ie brief (2025, on Shee et al. 2023) describes two marketing phases and claims RCC reduces lender default risk. Its extracted text gives no uptake percentages, so the 80%/20% figure may be in a figure or a later phase ([3ie](https://www.3ieimpact.org/sites/default/files/2025-07/TW13.1028-IE-brief.pdf)). The figures are still unreconciled. Whether they conflict at all, or are two phases, is unverified.
- **US weather-index-linked loans.** None found, only concept work (NIFA) and FDIC/NY Fed evidence that subsidized crop insurance already does this job for lenders.
