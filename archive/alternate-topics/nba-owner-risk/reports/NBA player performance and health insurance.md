# NBA owners self-insure most player risk

An NBA owner can move player health and performance risk through four families of tools: indemnity disability insurance, parametric and contingency insurance, clauses in the player contract, and traded hedges (prediction markets, sportsbooks and capital-markets structures). As of November 2026 only disability insurance and contract clauses are used at scale, and they leave most of the loss with the owner. Teams paid roughly $1.3 billion in 2025-26 salary to players who could not play, while the league's mandatory disability programme pays nothing until a player has missed 41 consecutive regular-season games and never covers luxury tax, playoff revenue or underperformance. Prediction markets cannot fill that gap today: no venue lists a games-missed or injury contract, the NBA and the CFTC's June 2026 rule proposal both oppose one, and the depth observed in season-long team markets supports positions of tens of thousands of dollars against eight-figure exposures. The most promising course project is therefore a parametric "short-absence" cover that pays salary times games missed below the league policy's 41-game threshold, priced from free injury and box-score data and compared at equal premium with a replica of the league policy. The most direct port of AgriHedge, a basis-risk back-test of win-total and make-playoffs contracts as hedges for injury loss, ranks second: it is feasible, but it will probably show a weak hedge. Hedging the "cost of success" (playoff bonuses and award-triggered raises) on Kalshi ranks third and has the only documented NBA precedent with prices. The evidence has one structural weakness: the league policy's terms come from press reporting, no primary policy document was reached, and no current premium was found for any NBA disability, revenue or performance cover.

*How to read the evidence tags.* This report synthesises five sets of research notes; no source was re-opened for it. A claim with no tag was read on the cited page by the researchers. "(search summary)" means the fact was seen only in a search-result summary and the page was not opened, so treat it as a lead. "(derived)" means the researchers calculated the figure from cited numbers and no source states it.

## Owners paid about $1.3 billion for unavailable players, and salary times games missed is the cleanest index

The largest measurable exposure is salary paid for games a player does not play. A tally built from Spotrac's injury tracker puts 2025-26 guaranteed pay to injured, ill or resting players at **more than $1.3 billion**, with team totals running from **$16.1 million (Timberwolves) to $81.5 million (Pacers)** ([ClutchPoints](https://clutchpoints.com/nba/nba-stories/nba-teams-paid-stunning-1-3-billion-for-injured-players)). That is about $43 million per team on average (derived) and about 28% of the aggregate salary cap, the highest non-pandemic share since tracking began in 2016-17 ([Above the Break](https://abovethebreak.substack.com/p/the-nba-is-in-the-red-zone)). The loss is concentrated in stars: pay to the 30 highest earners for team games they missed rose from about $79 million in 2015-16 to more than $352 million in 2024-25 and was on pace to exceed $525 million in 2025-26, an early-season projection ([Washington Post via Philadelphia Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/sixers/joel-embiid-stephen-curry-nba-injuries-costs-20251211.html)). A single game missed by a player on the top maximum salary of $54,126,450 costs about $660,000 in salary (derived from the [2025-26 cap figures](https://www.hoopsrumors.com/2025/06/salary-cap-tax-line-set-for-2025-26-nba-season.html)). No official NBA figure exists for any of this. All totals are third-party tracker tallies, and the trackers disagree: Jeff Stotts's In Street Clothes count for 2024-25 is 6,779 games missed ([Front Office Sports](https://frontofficesports.com/nba-injury-crisis-puts-82-game-schedule-under-new-scrutiny/)), while the Spotrac-derived count for the same season is 7,625, because Spotrac includes rest and counts days on its list. One of the five researchers could not verify the Above the Break method and treated its numbers as unreliable; another read the page and used them. The conflict is unresolved.

The luxury tax multiplies this loss for high-payroll teams. The tax is a penalty per dollar of team payroll above a league-set line ($187,895,000 in 2025-26), and from 2025-26 the rates are $1.00, $1.25, $3.50 and $4.75 per dollar across successive brackets, or $3.00 to $6.75 for teams that paid tax in three of the previous four seasons ([Hoops Rumors](https://www.hoopsrumors.com/2024/11/hoops-rumors-glossary-luxury-tax-penalties-4.html)). Madison Square Garden Sports, the listed owner of the Knicks, states in its fiscal 2026 annual report that an injured player's salary keeps counting toward the tax for at least one year and that disability insurance does not cover the tax ([MSG Sports 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001636519/000162828026056812/msgs-20260630.htm)). A non-repeater team $20 million over the line owes about $46.7 million in tax and a repeater about $86.7 million (derived, assuming the bracket width grew with the cap, which no source confirmed). Owners respond by shedding salary instead of paying. After Jayson Tatum's Achilles tear, the Celtics cut a projected salary-plus-tax bill of $512 million to about $274 million, an analyst estimate ([CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/nba/news/why-celtics-are-trading-away-key-players-money-not-the-only-reason-for-jrue-holiday-kristaps-porzingis-deals)), and about half of the 14 teams projected as 2025-26 taxpayers ducked under the line, leaving seven teams owing $223.1 million as of April 2026 ([Hoops Rumors](https://www.hoopsrumors.com/2026/04/projected-nba-taxpayers-for-2025-26-2.html)). The tax component is a deterministic function of the team's payroll sheet, so it is exactly computable in advance.

On the revenue side, the money sits in playoff home dates, not regular-season wins. The only audited figures come from the league's largest market. MSG Sports reported that playoff-related revenue rose **$66.9 million** in fiscal 2026 with nine Knicks home playoff games in both years, so the gain came from later rounds and higher prices, and that playoff-related expenses rose **$49.5 million** against it ([MSG Sports 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001636519/000162828026056812/msgs-20260630.htm)). Analysts put a Knicks home playoff date at about $12 million (JPMorgan) and a Finals home game at about $20.3 million (Seaport), and estimated that early-round sweeps cost about $29 million by removing home dates ([Front Office Sports](https://frontofficesports.com/knicks-finals-money-tickets/)). A per-round ladder of roughly $8 million, $12 million, $17 million and $20 million per home game is attributed to the same analyst (search summary). By contrast, the best-cited academic estimate of revenue per regular-season win is $486,524 plus $358,241 for the prior season's win, in 2011 dollars, relayed second-hand and not checked against the paper ([Kyle Newcombe](https://kylenewcombe.substack.com/p/marginal-revenue-product-analysis)). No current peer-reviewed estimate was found, and no per-round playoff revenue was found for any small or mid-sized market.

Evidence that a star's absence cuts the team's own revenue is thin. The cleanest measurement is on the resale market, where an announced superstar absence lowers ticket prices by 4% to 22% and by 21% (about $73 a ticket) for LeBron James road games ([Kaplan 2022, Economic Inquiry](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:ecinqu:v:60:y:2022:i:3:p:1357-1381)). Those are losses to ticket holders and resellers. No study was found that measures the effect on a team's primary gate, local television ratings, sponsorship or merchandise, and none on the multi-season effect of a long absence on season-ticket renewals. Franchise value is an even weaker target. Sportico values the average team at $6.64 billion and 14.6 times revenue in October 2026, the Celtics at $8 billion after a season without Tatum ([ClutchPoints summary of Sportico](https://clutchpoints.com/nba/nba-stories/nba-news-warriors-reach-14-5-billion-valuation-no-1-spot-over-knicks-lakers)), while Forbes puts the average at about $6.1 billion from regional editions ([Forbes Italia](https://forbes.it/2026/10/17/squadre-nba-piu-preziose-2026)). The two outlets use different methods, and no peer-reviewed study with coefficients linking wins or injuries to value was found. Meanwhile the fixed floor under every owner has risen: national television money per team went from $103 million to $143 million a year under the 2025-26 deals, a league projection ([Hoops Rumors](https://www.hoopsrumors.com/2025/11/nba-projecting-14-3-billion-in-revenue-for-2025-26-season.html)), which is about 31% of the average team's $456 million revenue (derived). The performance-sensitive remainder is gate, premium seating, sponsorship and playoff dates.

Performance risk without injury splits into a downside and an upside. The downside is a guaranteed contract that stops producing. Payroll-per-win benchmarks of $3.35 million (2021-22) and $3.83 million (2022-23) ([TrueHoop](https://www.truehoop.com/p/the-dollar-value-of-a-winning-nba); [Above the Break](https://abovethebreak.substack.com/p/the-most-valuable-players-of-the)) imply that a maximum-salary player must produce roughly 11 to 13 wins to break even (derived). The hard-dollar trace appears when a team waives a player and spreads the remaining pay over later seasons: the Bucks owe Damian Lillard about $113 million over five seasons ([Washington Post via Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/sixers/joel-embiid-stephen-curry-nba-injuries-costs-20251211.html)), and MSG Sports booked $49.1 million of provisions for waivers, terminations and trades in fiscal 2025 and roughly zero in fiscal 2026 ([MSG Sports 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001636519/000162828026056812/msgs-20260630.htm)). No league-wide annual total for dead money was found. The upside cost is binary and award-driven: an All-NBA selection made Jalen Duren eligible for $287 million over five years instead of $239.3 million, about $9 million a year more, though that article's own figures are internally inconsistent ([Front Office Sports](https://frontofficesports.com/article/all-nba-nod-makes-jalen-duren-eligible-for-287m-deal/)). Ordinary incentive bonuses are now immaterial: four were earned in 2025-26, each under $2 million ([Front Office Sports](https://frontofficesports.com/nba-teams-lowest-luxury-tax-check-covid-19-pandemic/)). A second count of how many players held bonuses (53 against 36) conflicts with the first and was seen only in a search summary. Health and upside cost offset each other through the 65-game rule, which makes a player who misses 18 or more games normally ineligible for the awards that raise his pay ([Hoops Rumors](https://www.hoopsrumors.com/2026/04/nba-deems-luka-doncic-cade-cunningham-award-eligible.html), search summary).

| Exposure | Best dollar evidence | Candidate index | How well the index works |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salary for games missed | About $1.3B league-wide in 2025-26; $16M to $81.5M per team; about $660k per game for a top-max player (derived) | Salary per game times games missed | Public and contractual, but the team decides who is active |
| Luxury tax on unusable salary | $1.00 to $4.75 per marginal dollar; $3.00 to $6.75 for repeaters | Payroll against the tax line, bracket and repeater status | Exactly computable; teams dump salary in response |
| Playoff revenue | Knicks: +$66.9M revenue against +$49.5M expense; $12M to $20M per home date (analyst estimates) | Home playoff dates by round | Clean count; dollars per date known only for New York |
| Regular-season wins | Under $1M revenue per win (2011 dollars, second-hand) | Wins | Clean but small, and contaminated by tanking |
| Star-absence demand | 4% to 22% on resale ticket prices | Games missed by a named star | No measured link to the team's own revenue |
| Underperformance and dead money | Lillard about $113M stretched; MSG $49.1M one year, about zero the next | Shortfall in Win Shares, VORP or EPM | Metrics disagree; the coach controls minutes |
| Award-triggered raises | About $45M to $48M over five years per trigger (derived) | All-NBA, MVP or DPOY announcement | Binary, public, payout computable in advance |
| Franchise value | $6.1B (Forbes) to $6.64B (Sportico) average | Annual appraisal | Poor: no measurable short-run link to health or wins |

## The league policy starts paying at game 41, leaving short absences, tax and revenue uninsured

The NBA runs a league-administered temporary total disability programme, which reimburses a team for part of an injured player's salary. The best current description is a July 2025 Sportico article: each club must insure **at least four of its highest-paid players**; cover begins only after **41 consecutive missed regular-season games**, which can span two seasons; the benefit is **50% or 80% of salary** depending on the tier the team buys; and the payout was capped at roughly **$492,000 per game in 2024-25** ([Sportico via Yahoo Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/article/nba-stars-fall-teams-help-150000419.html)). Older descriptions that still circulate (top five players, 80%, a $175,000 per-game cap) reflect earlier versions of the programme; the mandate was six players before 1999 and five in 2003 and 2013 ([ESPN, 2003](http://www.espn.com/sportsbusiness/s/2003/0311/1521986.html); [ESPN Chicago, 2013](https://www.espn.com/blog/chicago/bulls/post/_/id/13432/insurance-helps-bulls-foot-roses-bill)). None of this comes from a policy document or the collective bargaining agreement. The current carrier, broker and premium are not public. The last named carriers are Trustmark (2003) and MetLife (2013), and the only premium figures are "4% of salaries" from 2013 ([The Economist via CFO.com](https://www.cfo.com/news/the-economics-of-sports-insurance-claim-game/666530/)) and an unsourced "4% to 7%" from a consumer blog ([PolicyAdvisor](https://www.policyadvisor.com/magazine/cost-to-insure-nba)). No evidence was found, in either direction, that the 2017 or 2023 labour agreements changed the terms.

The reported recoveries match the stated formula, which lends the press description some credibility. Indiana could recover up to $18.2 million of Tyrese Haliburton's $45.6 million salary and Boston just over $20 million of Tatum's $54.1 million ([Sportico via Yahoo Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/article/nba-stars-fall-teams-help-150000419.html)); the Bulls were set to recover $6.561 million of Derrick Rose's $16.4 million in 2012-13 ([ESPN Chicago](https://www.espn.com/blog/chicago/bulls/post/_/id/13432/insurance-helps-bulls-foot-roses-bill)). The arithmetic sets a hard ceiling: a player hurt on opening night returns at most about 40% of that season's salary to the team, anything under 41 consecutive games returns nothing, and the per-game cap binds for salaries above about $50.4 million (all derived). These are projections from program rules. No confirmed claim payment for 2025-26 or 2026-27 was found, and no source states total annual claims, so the insured share of the $1.3 billion cannot be computed.

Exclusions remove the players a team most wants to insure. The insurer can carve out a whole player or a body part: Joel Embiid's right foot was excluded, leaving the 76ers to pay $9 million over two seasons with no recovery ([FOX Sports](https://www.foxsports.com/stories/nba/insurance-wont-cover-joel-embiids-foot-injury-for-sixers)), and Amar'e Stoudemire's knees and Luol Deng's back were excluded under the league plan ([ESPN Chicago](https://www.espn.com/blog/chicago/bulls/post/_/id/13432/insurance-helps-bulls-foot-roses-bill)). The widely repeated figure of 14 named exclusions per season could not be verified in a primary source. Beyond the league pool, a specialty market centred on Lloyd's writes one-off contract cover, but such policies "carry higher premiums, many exclusions, and rarely run longer than three years" ([The Economist via CFO.com](https://www.cfo.com/news/the-economics-of-sports-insurance-claim-game/666530/)), which is shorter than a four- or five-year maximum contract. A working range of 2% to 7% of insured salary per year is a synthesis of dated and partly weak data points, not a market quote. Death is not uniformly covered either: the final $12.5 million year of Brandon Clarke's contract was not protected when he died in 2026 ([Front Office Sports](https://frontofficesports.com/brandon-clarkes-nba-contract-death/)).

Insurance in the NBA is cash relief only. The labour agreement's own tools relieve roster and payroll constraints, not cash: the disabled player exception lets a team add one replacement worth the lesser of half the injured player's salary or the mid-level exception ([Hoops Rumors](https://hoopsrumors.com/2024/08/hoops-rumors-glossary-disabled-player-exception-3.html)), $14.1 million for 2025-26, and a career-ending ruling removes salary from the books only a year after the player's last game ([Hoops Rumors](https://hoopsrumors.com/2019/11/mozgovs-remaining-salary-removed-from-magics-books.html)). The NFL, by contrast, turns insurance recoveries into salary-cap credit the following season ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/41274295/nfl-insurance-policies-star-players-aaron-rodgers-tua-tagovailoa-jared-goff-joe-burrow-christian-mccaffrey)). The researchers inferred the following gap map from the program terms; no source lists these gaps explicitly.

| Exposure | Covered today? | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Salary for the first 41 games of an absence | No | 41-consecutive-game waiting period |
| Intermittent absences (30 games, return, 30 more) | No | "Consecutive" requirement |
| Salary above the per-game cap | No | About $492,000 per game in 2024-25 |
| The 20% or 50% retained share | No | Tier structure |
| Players outside the insured top four | Only if the team opts in | Minimum-four mandate |
| Excluded body parts or players | No, unless supplemental cover is found | Embiid, Stoudemire, Deng |
| Playoff games missed | No | Trigger and benefit are defined on regular-season games (Sportico's wording; not confirmed in a policy text) |
| Rest and load management | No | Not a disability; fined under the Player Participation Policy |
| Underperformance without injury | No | Disability trigger only |
| Luxury tax on dead salary | No | MSG Sports 10-K |
| Lost gate, sponsorship or playoff revenue | No evidence of any placed NBA policy | Only a 2011 "key-man" concept |
| Death | Varies by team and contract | Clarke, 2026 |

Other leagues show designs the NBA lacks. NFL clubs buy bespoke per-contract policies with waiting periods as short as one missed game, at about $1 million to $2 million a year to insure $40 million to $50 million ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/41274295/nfl-insurance-policies-star-players-aaron-rodgers-tua-tagovailoa-jared-goff-joe-burrow-christian-mccaffrey)). FIFA's Club Protection Programme pays clubs a daily indemnity after a 28-day wait for up to 365 days when a player is hurt on national-team duty ([European Football Clubs](https://www.efcfootball.com/en/fifa-club-protection-programme)). Its per-player cap is given as €7.5 million in the technical bulletin (search summary) and as $7.5 million on the club-association page, a discrepancy the researchers did not resolve. The 2026 World Baseball Classic marks where insurability currently ends: the single broker declined players aged 37 or over and players classed as "chronic", including Francisco Lindor, Jose Altuve and Mike Trout ([ESPN via TSN](https://www.tsn.ca/mlb/article/how-insurance-mess-shaped-2026-world-baseball-classic-n1-48112059/)). Indemnity insurers refuse precisely the riskiest stars, which is the strongest argument for index-based alternatives.

### Insurers will cover a team's success far more readily than its failure

The contingency market, a small group of Lloyd's syndicates and specialists, routinely insures bonuses that a team owes when it wins, because a team cannot cheaply cause itself to win. The Game Point hedges give the only NBA price comparison: exchange prices of 6% and 2% against insurer quotes of 12% to 13% and 7% to 8% ([The Block](https://theblock.co/news/deals/2026-02-13-kalshi-inks-sports-hedging-deal-with-game-point-on-the-heels-of-over-1-billion-in-super-bowl-trading-389841)), which implies insurer loadings of about two times market odds on the likelier event and three and a half to four times on the unlikelier one (derived). Cover against a team's own failure is much dearer and comes with conditions. One source said Dortmund paid close to 30% of the insured amount; its underwriters could cancel if the coach or three top players left, and they required that only three executives know the policy existed so that players would not ease off. An underwriter quoted in the same report said "the moral risk can be significant" and that he would sooner insure a sponsor than a club ([Insurance Journal / Bloomberg](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/international/2015/04/23/365411.htm)). Capacity is small as well: one of the main contingency underwriters helps cover upwards of $100 million of such risk a year across all clients ([JohnWallStreet](https://www.johnwallstreet.com/p/rising-costs-driving-demand-for-contractual-bonus-revenue-protection-insurance)), about two top maximum salaries for a single season. LongBall's baseball product is the one real precedent for insuring performance itself, and its price of about 10% of salary for up to 60% reimbursement is the only benchmark for a trigger wider than injury. Its status since October 2024 is unknown and its details rest on a single article.

### Parametric cover and contract clauses already use games missed as an index

The Zurich and Players Health college policy is the only live games-missed product found. It pays a school's contractual payments to an athlete who suffers a listed injury and misses a significant portion of the season, "typically 40% or more" ([Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/midwest/2026/01/06/853263.htm)), and premiums are set from an injury probability estimated per athlete ([Carrier Management](https://www.carriermanagement.com/news/2025/06/05/275720.htm), search summary). The two conditions do different jobs: the medical condition stops the buyer from manufacturing a claim by resting a player, and the games-missed count makes the amount objective. NBA contracts already contain privately negotiated versions of the same index, with the player in the insurer's seat. Zion Williamson's 2022 extension reportedly ties future guarantees to games played, with tiers at 41, 51 and 61 games ([Sports Illustrated](https://www.si.com/nba/2023/12/21/zion-williamson-contract-no-longer-guaranteed-final-three-years-clause), search summary; sources differ on details), and Embiid's 2017 extension let the 76ers shed salary after specified foot or back injuries, the same body parts insurers had excluded. Clauses and insurance are therefore complements: clauses cover known conditions on players with weak bargaining power, insurance covers new injuries to healthy stars, and neither covers the first 41 games of a new injury to a fully guaranteed player. Threshold designs carry a known flaw. The Mets recovered 75% of David Wright's salary once he had missed 60 games and then kept him on the disabled list ([Fangraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/david-wright-peter-alonso-and-the-law-of-metropolitan-frugality), search summary). A payout that grows linearly per game missed, with a deductible and a retained share, spreads that incentive instead of concentrating it at one game count.

### Exchanges list every NBA contract except the one an owner needs

Kalshi, the deepest US venue, lists game winners, spreads, totals, player statistics, playoff qualification, conference and title winners and season awards ([RotoWire](https://www.rotowire.com/prediction-markets/nba)), and a win-total ladder for every team (search summary). **No games-played, games-missed or injury contract was found on any US sportsbook or prediction market**, and the regulatory direction is against one. The NBA asked the CFTC in April 2026 to prohibit markets on injuries as "readily susceptible to manipulation and/or improper use of confidential information" ([Washington Post via Anchorage Daily News](https://www.adn.com/sports/national-sports/2026/07/23/the-hottest-market-on-kalshi-is-one-the-nba-doesnt-think-should-exist/)). The CFTC's proposed rule of 10 June 2026 disfavours player-injury contracts and treats aggregate team outcomes more favourably ([Akin Gump](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/alerts/cftc-proposes-new-framework-for-regulating-prediction-markets), search summary); no final rule was found as of 11 November 2026. Since October 2026 the NBA has also barred low-probability "under" bets on individual players at sportsbooks and at its prediction-market partners ([Front Office Sports](https://frontofficesports.com/article/nba-draws-new-lines-fans-bet/)). Per-game player contracts are no substitute, because sportsbook props are generally void when the player does not play ([Action Network](https://www.actionnetwork.com/education/prop-betting-rules-what-happens-if-player-doesnt-play), search summary); Kalshi's own rule for that case was not verified. The legal footing of all sports contracts is also split: one federal appeals court ruled for Kalshi and another held on 25 September 2026 that its sports contracts are not swaps, which lets Ohio and Tennessee enforce their gambling laws ([SBC Americas](https://sbcamericas.com/2026/09/25/kalshi-sixth-circuit-sides-oh-tn/), search summary). The usable proxies are team-level contracts and "no" positions on a player's awards, which lose value as the 65-game threshold slips away.

Depth, not fees, is the binding constraint. Kalshi's most-traded NBA game on one late-October 2026 Sunday did $15.2 million of volume ([Covers](https://www.covers.com/industry/kalshi-generates-10-billion-of-14-billion-in-weekend-trading-oct-27-2026)), but the season-long contracts an owner would use are thin: one make-playoffs contract traded 1,354 contracts in 24 hours ([SportsGrid](https://www.sportsgrid.com/nba/article/nba-kalshi-update-jan-17-bucks-playoff-odds-crash-jokic-mvp-drop), search summary) and a conference-seed contract was quoted 6 cents bid, 19 cents offered ([SportsGrid](https://www.sportsgrid.com/nba/article/daily-nba-prediction-markets-wrapped-kalshi-market-movers-10), search summary). Kalshi's basketball contract terms set a position accountability level of **$25,000 per strike, per member** ([Kalshi contract terms](https://kalshi-public-docs.s3.amazonaws.com/contract_terms/BASKETBALLPOINTSRACE.pdf)); that figure was read only in the points-race contract, it is not stated as a hard cap, and no hard limit was found. A $10 million payout from a contract trading at 30 cents needs 10 million contracts and $3 million of premium, thousands of days of that observed turnover (derived). The largest pool of NBA open interest found, $74.9 million in the championship market, is the size of one star's salary and spread across 30 teams ([DeFi Rate](https://defirate.com/news/kalshi-posts-record-3-9b-volume-extends-lead-over-polymarket-parlays-surge/), search summary). Fees are small by comparison, about 0.07 times price times one minus price per contract ([prediction.com](https://prediction.com/blog/kalshi-fees-complete-guide-2026)), though secondary sources disagree on whether the sports multiplier is 7% or about 6% and on maker fees, and the official schedule could not be loaded. Other venues add little: Kalshi held about $10 billion of a $14 billion weekend, and none of the others lists a different kind of contract. No example was found of a team, league or owner hedging salary or injury exposure through betting or prediction markets, and no evidence was found of block-trading facilities for NBA season-long contracts.

Market prices are still useful as inputs. NBA closing lines absorb player-absence news by the close ([Dare, Dennis and Paul 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finlet:v:13:y:2015:i:c:p:130-136), search summary), and sports prices on Kalshi track realised frequencies closely at horizons of a week or less ([Kagan and Baiocchi 2026](https://www2.gwu.edu/~forcpgm/2026-001.pdf), search summary). Cheap contracts lose money after fees and bias grows at long horizons ([Bürgi, Deng and Whelan 2026](https://www.ucd.ie/economics/t4media/WP2025_19.pdf), search summary), which is where "this team has a bad season" payoffs sit. A hedge must also be in place before injury news, since the news is priced within the day.

### Basis risk and moral hazard trade off one for one

The central difference from a weather or crop index is that both parties control the NBA index. The league fined the Jazz $500,000 and the Pacers $100,000 in February 2026 for holding out players who could have played ([ESPN](https://africa.espn.com/nba/story/_/id/47914175/nba-fines-jazz-500k-pacers-100k-sitting-players), search summary), and a baseball study finds players in contract years spend fewer games on the injured list ([StudyFinds summary of Paulsen](https://studyfinds.com/mlb-players-return-from-injuries-faster-when-contracts-about-to-expire/), search summary). Games missed is partly a choice. The more closely an index follows the owner's own players, the lower the basis risk and the easier it is to manipulate; the researchers' expected ordering runs from player-specific games missed (lowest basis risk, highest moral hazard) through salary-weighted team games lost and team wins to a league-wide injury index. That ordering is an inference. **No published estimate exists of the basis risk between an NBA owner's loss and any candidate index.** The agricultural literature the team already knows shows why it must be measured: a flagship Kenyan livestock index product left buyers with about 69% of their catastrophic risk ([Jensen, Barrett and Mude](https://agris.fao.org/search/ru/records/672364098aa2fad1ce470c9a), search summary), and the World Bank's proposed test is the probability of no payout when the buyer's loss is severe ([World Bank WPS7744](https://financialprotectionforum.org/sites/default/files/WPS7744.pdf), search summary). Adverse selection has a standard answer in the league programme itself: cover is mandatory for a rule-defined set of players, so a team cannot insure only the ones it privately knows are fragile. A new voluntary product should copy that rule.

## Free data can price games missed, but not injury dates or what teams privately know

A student team can assemble the needed panel at no cost. The pipeline used by a September 2026 NBER working paper is the most defensible: injury events from Pro Sports Transactions, games missed from NBA.com box scores, each cross-checked against the other, giving 251,037 player-game observations and 6,570 time-loss injury events for 2017-18 to 2022-23 ([Krishna, Lychagin and McLean, NBER WP 35803](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35803/w35803.pdf)).

| Prediction-market history | Kalshi API | Live tier of about three months, older data from `/historical` endpoints ([Kalshi docs](https://docs.kalshi.com/getting_started/historical_data), search summary) | Docs failed to load; authentication and first NBA market date unverified |

Data quality dictates what the contract can settle on. An audit of 180 records found the public injury data correct on injury date only 44.7% of the time, on games missed 84.1% and on injury site 95.6% ([Bullock et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8207289)). Games played is exactly observable from box scores. A payoff defined on games missed is therefore far easier to price and settle than one defined on injury type or date. The definition must still separate injury from other absences: in the NBER sample, 44,734 player-games were missed by coach decision against 40,444 for injury ([NBER WP 35803](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35803/w35803.pdf)). Access to salary history from Spotrac, HoopsHype or Basketball Reference contract pages was not verified, and Spotrac's team injury table returned a paywall.

The base rates point to a specific model shape. Time-loss injuries run at about 15.6 per 1,000 player-games in 2008-19 data, with a median of 3 games missed, 59% of injuries costing three games or fewer and 15% costing 14 or more ([Bullock et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8207289)). The NBER sample implies about 41 events per 1,000 (derived), with a median of 2 games missed and a mean of 6.2; the gap likely reflects fuller capture and a later era, and the team should compute its own rate. Star players missed an average of 10.6 games a season in the 1990s and 23.9 in the 2020s, per a league-commissioned study ([NBA.com](https://www.nba.com/news/nba-sends-data-load-management-study)), so older seasons understate today's expected loss. Achilles ruptures and ACL tears carry return rates of roughly 72% to 89%, absences of ten to twelve months and about 20% lower production in the first season back (search summaries of several cohort studies, including [Chauhan et al. 2021](https://www.pubmed.gov/34010095/)). No source gives the full distribution of games missed per player-season, or the annual frequency of season-ending injuries; the team would have to compute both.

Prediction from public data is weak, and that matters for adverse selection. Published models reach about 0.73 to 0.84 on the standard discrimination score (AUC), with the top figure from a study of lower-body muscle strains ([Lu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9340342), search summary). Figures above 0.9 are regarded as suspect, calibration is rarely reported, and none of the papers the researchers saw validates on later seasons than it trained on. The NBER paper finds that minutes causally raise injury risk once coach selection is accounted for, about 0.1 percentage point per additional minute for a star, and that injury risk is higher after a coach rests a player than after an illness, which the authors read as coaches acting on private knowledge ([NBER WP 35803](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35803/w35803.pdf)). The league's own study, by contrast, "did not see a lower injury rate when players rested"; the two readings conflict and the question is open. Sources also disagree on whether age predicts injury: one trainer-data study found no correlation ([Drakos et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3445097), search summary), while the league study lists age, prior injury and prior surgery as risk factors. The practical signal for a season-long contract is slow-moving: prior injury history, age and expected minutes.

No published paper prices an NBA injury contract with actuarial methods. The nearest work multiplies a per-game injury probability by salary ([Wang, Wharton](https://wsb.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/WANG_Using-Injury-Risk-Forecasting-to-Quantify-Financial-Impact-in-the-NBA-1.pdf)), and that paper has visible flaws. The method ladder the researchers recommend is therefore their own inference from the base rates. It starts with a burn-rate price (the historical average payoff within buckets of age, prior games missed and minutes), which is the baseline any model must beat. Next comes a hurdle or zero-inflated count model of games missed per player-season, then a frequency model times a heavy-tailed severity model run by Monte Carlo, which is needed for deductibles, caps and thresholds such as 65 games. Validation should train on seasons up to a given year and test on the next, reporting Brier score and calibration by decile, which is stricter than most of the published literature. The risks are not independent: compressed schedules raised injury rates for every team at once, to 21.68 per 1,000 in the 2011-12 lockout season against 13.54 in 2008-09 ([Bullock et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8207289)) and by a ratio of 1.42 in 2020-21 ([Morikawa et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9445480)). No study decomposes injury variance into player, team and season components, and none measures how a star's injury moves win totals or playoff odds.

Performance apart from health is harder to contract on. A play-by-play impact score had a year-to-year correlation of 0.242 against 0.75 for the box-score composite PER ([Deshpande and Jensen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.03186)), so a contract on a pure plus-minus metric would pay out mostly on noise. Volume metrics such as Win Shares and VORP mix performance with minutes played, so a contract on them is partly an injury contract. No published projection-error sizes for modern metrics were found.

## Five buildable projects, ranked, with a short-absence parametric cover first

The ranking weighs four things: whether free data supports the build in one semester, whether the result is informative whichever way it comes out, how much of AgriHedge's method carries over, and how much rests on unverified facts. The first two ideas share one data panel and fit together as a single project, with the first as the core and the second as the market comparator.

### 1. A parametric cover for the first 41 games, compared at equal premium with the league policy

The team would build a player-season panel of games missed to injury and salary, code the league policy's payoff as reported (41 consecutive games, 80%, per-game cap), and design a parametric alternative that pays salary per game times games missed below that threshold, for a rule-defined set such as each team's four highest salaries. It would price both with the same loading, starting from burn-rate prices and moving to a frequency-severity simulation, and compare them at equal premium on the share of the owner's worst-decile losses each covers and on the probability of no payout in a bad year. Payout shapes (linear per game, stepped, with deductibles and retained shares) map directly onto AgriHedge's contract-design work. The data are Pro Sports Transactions, box scores and a salary table. The project could show three things no source provides: the share of injured-player salary that falls in absences too short for the league policy to reach, the fair premium for that layer as a percentage of salary, and the distribution of games missed per player-season. The main risk is data assembly and definition. Separating injury from rest is clean only from 2021-22, salary-history access was not verified, and the league terms being replicated are press-reported. Restricting the insured set to top salaries should shrink the coach-decision problem, since bench players account for most such absences, but that is an assumption to check in the panel. Moral hazard can be argued from precedent but not tested.

### 2. A basis-risk back-test of win-total and make-playoffs contracts as an injury hedge

This is AgriHedge with the crop replaced by a roster. For each team-season since 2003, the team would take the preseason win total from Basketball Reference, the realised wins and the realised injured-salary loss from the first project's panel, and estimate how much of the loss a preseason "under" position offsets, the hedge ratio, and the probability the hedge pays nothing in a worst-decile injury year. About 23 seasons of 30 teams gives enough to estimate a hedge ratio with wide error bars. A second part would pull live Kalshi order books for win-total, make-playoffs and award contracts and measure the size obtainable against a $50 million exposure. The result would be the first measurement of this basis risk and of the capacity gap. The main risk is that the answer is a weak hedge, which is a legitimate finding but a less satisfying product. In-season futures history is scarce, so any dynamic hedge would be a small-sample illustration, Kalshi's NBA history is short, and Basketball Reference was confirmed to carry the totals but not the prices on each side.

### 3. Exchange hedges for the cost of success, priced against insurer loadings

The team would take the liabilities that pay on public binary events (a bonus for making the playoffs or winning a round, and the roughly $9 million a year raise triggered by an All-NBA, MVP or Defensive Player of the Year award), build each hedge from the matching Kalshi contract, and compare its all-in cost, including the bid-offer spread at realistic size, with the insurer quotes from the Game Point trades. A simple model probability from win totals or team ratings would test whether long-dated contract prices are biased. The data are Kalshi's API, preseason odds and award histories. The project could show whether the exchange's apparent two- to four-fold price advantage survives spreads as wide as 13 cents, and it demonstrates a hedge with almost no basis risk. The main risks are sample size and existence: the insurer side rests on two anonymous trades, Kalshi's listed awards are MVP, Defensive Player, Rookie and Coach of the Year and an All-NBA contract was not confirmed, and only one player's pay tier changed with the 2026 All-NBA announcement.

### 4. A league pool or "availability bond" for the short-absence layer

Using the first project's panel, the team would simulate all 30 teams pooling the first-41-games layer, estimate how much pooling cuts each team's volatility, add a season-wide shock calibrated to 2011-12 and 2020-21, and slice the pooled loss into layers with attachment probabilities in the manner of a catastrophe bond. The result would show how much diversification the league-wide factor destroys and what capital a mutual would need. It depends entirely on the first project, it is simulation with no price benchmark to validate against, and no precedent exists beyond the 2003 FIFA cancellation bond. It works best as an extension to the first project.

### 5. A performance-shortfall warranty modelled on LongBall

The team would project each player's next-season value from a weighted average of recent seasons plus an age adjustment, define a payout when realised value falls below a threshold, price it, and compare the price with LongBall's roughly 10% of salary for up to 60% reimbursement. The data are Basketball Reference metrics and the RAPTOR archive. The project could show how wide projection errors are and how much of a "performance" payout is really missed games. It ranks last because the index is contestable, the coach controls minutes and role far more directly than in baseball, the only price benchmark is a single article about a single policy, and no market instrument exists to compare against, so little of AgriHedge's tooling carries over.

Three directions are not worth building. A market-traded contract on one player's injuries or games played does not exist and regulators disfavour it. Sportsbook player props void when the player sits and are limited to a few thousand dollars. Franchise value has no measurable short-run link to health or wins.

## Conclusion

The pivot inverts AgriHedge's structure. In agriculture the index is exogenous and the traded contract exists, so the work is assembling a hedge and measuring its basis risk. In the NBA the index that tracks the loss best, games missed, is partly chosen by the insured and is the one contract exchanges will not list. A sports version of the project therefore shifts its weight from building a hedge out of listed contracts to designing the contract that is not listed, with market prices serving as a comparator and a probability input. A team that pivots expecting Kalshi to carry the hedge will mostly document why it cannot; a team that pivots to contract design has an unoccupied gap, a real precedent in the college product, and several numbers that no published source reports.

Three facts would change this picture and are worth checking before committing. A final CFTC rule could settle whether any player-level availability contract can ever be listed. Evidence that the CME team-index futures launched and trade would add an instrument built for exactly this use, though for hockey only. And a primary source for the league policy's terms would turn the equal-premium comparison in the first project from a replica of press reports into a replica of the actual contract.
